Semantic retrieval over enterprise content

Modules 3 and 4 answered questions from tables and from a graph. Most of what an enterprise knows about its own rules is written in documents: policies, procedures, tariffs. This module builds the component that finds the right passage in them, for the person who is allowed to read it, in the version that is in force, and measures how often it succeeds.

Case study: bank product-policy search for contact-centre staff Lab tools: PostgreSQL with pgvector in Docker, Python 3.13, OpenAI embeddings, a local reranker Hands-on: six exercises in section 5.8
5.0

From facts to passages

A contact-centre agent has a customer on the line who wants to close a fixed deposit early. The penalty is in a policy document, in a paragraph that calls it "premature withdrawal". The agent types "penalty for breaking an FD".

Nothing in Modules 3 and 4 answers this. The penalty is one sentence in a document, and the question uses none of that sentence's words. The reference architecture of Module 1 gives the job to the retrieval component, which returns passages as evidence for a person or for an answering model.

Retrieval over enterprise content differs from retrieval over a public benchmark in four ways, and each has a section here. Documents have versions, and an answer from a superseded version is wrong. Documents have audiences, and a passage shown to the wrong role is a breach. Much of the content is in tables. And the organization has a glossary (Module 2) that says which words mean the same thing, which a general-purpose model does not know.

The lab bank, Lakemont Bank, is fictional. Its products, charges, forms and policies are invented for this module and are no real bank's terms.

Learning outcomes

After this module you should be able to:

  1. distinguish similarity, relevance, evidence sufficiency, factual correctness and faithfulness, and say which component each one tests;
  2. design an ingestion pipeline that handles tables, enriches chunks with glossary terms, keeps versions straight, re-indexes incrementally, removes deleted content and inherits access control;
  3. choose an embedding model and a dimension by measuring on your own questions, and estimate what the choice costs to embed, store and change;
  4. implement hybrid retrieval with a reranker in one PostgreSQL engine and explain what full-text search and vector search each miss;
  5. select among six retrieval architectures from the failure each one fixes and the cost it adds;
  6. evaluate retrieval with recall, precision, MRR, nDCG and evidence sufficiency, report a confidence interval, and state what a 60-question set can and cannot show;
  7. diagnose the ten worst queries of a system, change two architecture choices in response and record the decision.

5.1

Five different things

"Is the search accurate?" can mean five things. They are measured at different points, by different methods, and a system can pass one while failing the next.

Take the question "What is the late payment charge on a Gold Credit Card when 15,000 rupees is due?" and follow it through a system that retrieves passages and then writes an answer from them.

Similarity is cheap and is all that a vector index computes. Relevance is what users want and can only be established by people, which is why a gold set exists. A set of relevant passages can still be insufficient, as in the table example.

An answer can be faithful to its passages and false, when the passages are out of date: the version 3 answer above quotes its source exactly. And an answer can be true without being faithful, when the model answered from what it learned in training and the passages happened not to contradict it. That last case passes a correctness check today and fails on the day the policy changes.

The first three belong to retrieval and are this module's subject. Correctness and faithfulness belong to the answering step and return in Module 6, where an assistant writes answers from metrics, graph facts and passages. The reason to separate them now is diagnostic. When an answer is wrong, the first question is whether sufficient evidence was retrieved. If it was, the fault is in generation. If it was not, no prompt will fix it.

Answer relevance is a sixth term you will meet, in the Ragas framework and elsewhere: whether the answer addresses the question that was asked, regardless of truth. An answer that correctly states the annual fee is irrelevant to a question about the late payment charge.

5.2

Enterprise ingestion

Retrieval can only return what ingestion stored. Seven decisions are made before the first question is asked, and most retrieval failures in the lab trace back to one of them.

Parsing and chunking

Parsing turns a file into text with its structure kept: title, headings, paragraphs, lists, tables. A is the unit that is embedded, indexed and returned. Its size is a trade. A small chunk gives a precise vector, because it is about one thing, and may leave out the sentence that makes it intelligible. A large chunk carries its context and has a vector that averages several topics. The lab's pipeline makes one chunk per paragraph and keeps, on every chunk, the whole section it came from, so that the choice can be changed at query time (section 5.5).

Where the text comes from matters more than the chunking rule. The lab's documents are Markdown with a metadata header, which parses reliably. PDF, scanned forms and slide decks need a layout-aware parser, and its errors (columns read across, headers repeated on every page, tables flattened into a run of numbers) reach the index unnoticed unless a sample of parsed output is read by a person.

Tables and structured content

A fee schedule is a table, and the answer to "what is the charge when 12,000 is due" is one cell of it. Embedded as raw Markdown, a table row such as | 5,001 to 20,000 | 750 | has no meaning without the header row, and a table split across two chunks loses the header for the second half. The lab's pipeline keeps a table as one chunk and writes each row with its column names.

A range lookup shows the limit of retrieval. Neither search method knows that 12,000 lies between 5,001 and 20,000. Retrieval can return the table, and the reading of it is left to the person or the answering model.

Metadata enrichment with glossary terms

Each chunk is stored with metadata: the document it came from, its version and effective date, its section, whether it is a table, who may read it, and the glossary terms it mentions. The glossary is the one from Module 2. It records that "premature withdrawal" is the preferred term and that staff and customers also say "breaking a deposit" and "early closure". The lab tags each chunk with the terms it mentions and adds their synonyms to the chunk's full-text index, so that a question using a synonym matches a paragraph that uses the preferred term.

Three things follow from the tags. Full-text search finds the paragraph by any of its names. The term identifiers can filter or group results. And the link runs both ways: when the glossary owner changes a definition, the chunks that mention the term can be listed for review.

Document versions

A policy is re-issued, and for a period both versions exist: the old one in people's bookmarks and possibly in the index. If both are indexed, the two tables are almost identical text with almost identical vectors, and nothing in similarity prefers the current one.

There are three workable designs. Keep only the current version in the search index and archive the others elsewhere, which the lab does. Keep all versions, flag one as current, and filter on the flag in every query. Or keep all versions with their validity periods and filter on a date, which is needed when staff must answer "what was the charge in March" and repeats the as-of pattern of Module 4. What does not work is to index every version and leave the choice to ranking.

Incremental indexing and deletions

Rebuilding the index for every change costs an embedding call per chunk and, with an approximate index, a rebuild. embeds only what changed. The lab computes a hash for each chunk from everything that affects retrieval: its search text, its glossary synonyms, its entitlement tags and the name of the embedding model. On a refresh, a chunk whose key and hash are unchanged keeps its vector. A changed or new chunk is embedded. A chunk that the new file no longer produces is deleted. Version 4 of the Gold card policy changes two of nine chunks, so the refresh makes two embedding calls.

Deletion needs its own path, because a document that is withdrawn sends no new file. The lab reads a list of withdrawn document identifiers and removes their rows. Until it does, the withdrawn festive deposit offer is returned for two gold questions, with a rate the bank no longer pays. A source system that can only say "these are the documents that exist now" is handled by comparing that list with the index and deleting the difference. Where regulation requires proof of removal, a row records what was removed and when.

The hash includes the model name because vectors from two models cannot be compared. A change of embedding model, or of what is put in front of each chunk before embedding, changes every hash, and the next refresh embeds the whole corpus. Lab 6 shows this when you switch on contextual headers.

Access-control inheritance

Each source document has an audience. The fraud playbook is for fraud analysts and team leads, and it says, among other things, that a caller must not be told a fraud hold has been placed. A chunk cut from it inherits its audience as an , and every query filters on the tag inside the database. The roles in that filter come from the server, which looks them up for the signed-in user. A role sent by the browser or typed into a request is never trusted. In the lab a script passes the role, because there is no login.

Where the filter is appliedWhat happensVerdict

Three rules make inheritance safe. Fail closed: a document with no audience is refused at ingestion, because a filter cannot exclude a row that has no tag. Propagate changes: when a document's audience is narrowed at the source, its chunks must be retagged at once, so the audience is part of the content hash. And test it: the gold set contains questions asked by a role that may not read the answer, and the expected result is that no restricted chunk comes back. The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications lists this class of fault as LLM08:2025, vector and embedding weaknesses, and recommends permission-aware vector stores.

With an approximate vector index a filter has a cost. pgvector applies the WHERE condition after the index scan, so a condition that keeps 10 percent of rows leaves about 4 of the 40 candidates the index returns by default. Since version 0.8.0, iterative index scans continue the scan until enough rows pass the filter. Section 5.4 returns to this.

Four questions decide the choice, in this order.

Where may the text go? A hosted API receives every chunk and every question. If policy documents may not leave the bank's environment, the candidates are open-weight models on the bank's own hardware or a hosted model inside the bank's cloud tenancy.

Which model retrieves best on your questions? Public leaderboards rank models on public datasets. Your corpus has parallel products, internal codes and house vocabulary. The only reliable comparison is to index your corpus with each candidate and run your gold set, which is what the lab's adapter is for: the scripts take their vectors from OpenAI's model or from a local one, depending on whether OPENAI_API_KEY is set, with EMBED_PROVIDER to override it.

What does the dimension cost? pgvector stores a vector in 4 bytes per dimension plus 8, so a 1,536-dimension vector takes about 6 KB and ten million of them take about 61 GB before any index. Its HNSW and IVFFlat indexes accept the vector type up to 2,000 dimensions and the half-precision halfvec type up to 4,000, so a 3,072-dimension vector must be shortened or stored as halfvec to be indexed. Models trained with representation learning let you keep the first part of the vector: OpenAI's guide states that text-embedding-3-large shortened to 256 dimensions still scores above the older ada-002 at 1,536 on the MTEB benchmark.

What does a change cost? The price of embedding is small. OpenAI's guide lists 62,500 pages per dollar for text-embedding-3-small and 9,615 for text-embedding-3-large, which at about 800 tokens a page is USD 0.02 and USD 0.13 per million tokens. The expensive event is a change of model, because every vector must be recomputed and the index rebuilt while the old one keeps serving. Budget for that at the start, since the model you choose will be superseded.

Two practical rules. Embed queries and documents the way the model card says: a model trained with prefixes or an input_type loses quality without them, and the loss is silent. And record the model name and dimension with the index, as the lab does in its content hash and its index_runs table.

Vector search and full-text search fail on different questions. Running both and fusing the two rankings is called , and PostgreSQL with the pgvector extension can do it in one statement.

Full-text search in PostgreSQL

stores each chunk as a tsvector: its words reduced to stems, without stop words, with positions. "Charges" and "charged" both become charg. A question becomes a tsquery, and the @@ operator tests the match. A GIN index makes the test fast.

Two properties of the built-in functions shape the lab's query. First, plainto_tsquery joins the words of its input with AND. A question of twelve words almost never finds a paragraph containing all of them, so the lab turns the ANDs into ORs and lets ranking sort the candidates.

Second, ts_rank and ts_rank_cd score a document from how often and how close together the query words appear in that document. They use no statistics about the collection, so a word that appears in every policy counts as much as a word that appears in one. This is weaker than the BM25 ranking of a search engine, which weights rare words more. The PostgreSQL documentation calls its two ranking functions examples and invites you to write your own. Extensions and dedicated engines provide BM25. Fusion and the reranker compensate for part of the difference.

The full-text search of the lab (lab03_hybrid.py)
WITH q AS (SELECT to_tsquery('english',
             replace(plainto_tsquery('english', %(qtext)s)::text, ' & ', ' | ')) AS t)
SELECT chunk_key, row_number() OVER (ORDER BY ts_rank_cd(tsv, q.t) DESC, chunk_key) AS rank
FROM chunks, q
WHERE tsv @@ q.t
  AND allowed_roles && %(roles)s::text[]                               -- entitlement filter
  AND (%(docs)s::text[] IS NULL OR doc_id = ANY(%(docs)s::text[]))       -- optional document filter
ORDER BY ts_rank_cd(tsv, q.t) DESC, chunk_key
LIMIT %(n)s

Vector search with pgvector

pgvector adds a vector column type and distance operators. <=> is cosine distance, <-> Euclidean distance and <#> negative inner product. Ordering by the operator and taking the first rows is a nearest-neighbour search. Without an index the search is exact: PostgreSQL computes the distance to every row. That is the right choice for the lab's 127 chunks and stays practical into the tens of thousands.

Beyond that an approximate index is used. builds a layered graph of neighbours and searches it greedily. Its build parameters are m (connections per node, 16 by default) and ef_construction (64), and the query parameter hnsw.ef_search (40) sets how many candidates a search keeps. A higher value finds more of the true neighbours and takes longer.

IVFFlat, the other index type, clusters the vectors and searches the nearest clusters: it builds faster, uses less memory, and has lower recall at the same speed. An approximate index adds a second kind of miss, on top of the model's: the right chunk is close to the question and the index does not reach it. The optional script hnsw_demo.py measures this on the lab corpus.

The vector search of the lab, and the index statement for a large table
SELECT chunk_key, row_number() OVER (ORDER BY embedding <=> %(qvec)s::vector) AS rank
FROM chunks
WHERE allowed_roles && %(roles)s::text[]
  AND (%(docs)s::text[] IS NULL OR doc_id = ANY(%(docs)s::text[]))
ORDER BY embedding <=> %(qvec)s::vector
LIMIT %(n)s;

-- not created in the lab; hnsw_demo.py creates it, measures it and drops it
CREATE INDEX chunks_hnsw ON chunks USING hnsw (embedding vector_cosine_ops) WITH (m = 16, ef_construction = 64);

Fusing two rankings

The two searches return scores on unrelated scales: a cosine distance between 0 and 2, and a cover-density rank with no upper bound. Adding them is meaningless. uses only positions. Each chunk scores 1 / (k + rank) in each list it appears in, and the scores are summed. Cormack, Clarke and Buettcher introduced it in 2009 with k = 60, and that value is still the common default. A chunk ranked first by one search and absent from the other scores 1/61. A chunk ranked fifth by both scores 2/65, which is higher. Agreement between the methods is rewarded over a single high rank.

The constant k controls how much the top positions dominate. With k = 1, first place (1/2) is worth one and a half times second place (1/3). With k = 60 the first ten places are worth nearly the same, and appearing in both lists decides the order.

The reranker

An embedding model is a : it reads the question and the passage separately and compares two vectors. That is what makes search over millions of passages possible, and it is also a limit, because everything about a passage has to fit in one vector computed before any question was known. A reads the question and one passage together and outputs a relevance score. It can attend to the exact words that matter for this question. It cannot be precomputed, so it is applied only to the top candidates of the fused list: 20 in the lab.

The lab's reranker is a small open model, ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2, run on the laptop's CPU. Hosted rerankers from Cohere and Voyage, and larger open models such as bge-reranker-v2-m3, are the alternatives. The cost is latency. In the run recorded for this page, hybrid search took about 25 ms at the median and hybrid search with reranking about 870 ms, on a CPU.

The second cost is less obvious. A reranker judges the text it is given. In the baseline run, reranking raised nDCG@5 from 0.762 to 0.811 and lowered evidence sufficiency from 0.891 to 0.836: many chunks do not name their product, and the reranker preferred the same paragraph of a sibling product. With the document title and heading in front of each chunk (section 5.5), reranking raised both, nDCG@5 from 0.863 to 0.954 and sufficiency from 0.945 to 0.964. Each of these differences is a handful of questions out of 55, so treat the direction as a finding to check on your own corpus. Lab 5 shows the individual questions.

What one engine buys, and where it stops

Keeping text, metadata, full-text index and vectors in one PostgreSQL has four concrete advantages. A refresh is one transaction, so the vector and the text of a chunk cannot disagree. The entitlement filter is one WHERE condition that both searches share. Results join to any other table, such as the documents table that the lab uses to find product names. And the bank already knows how to back up, secure and audit PostgreSQL.

It stops being enough when full-text ranking quality matters more than the lab shows (a search engine with BM25, phrase boosts and analyzers per language), when the vector count reaches hundreds of millions, or when query volume needs the search tier scaled separately from the database. Those are reasons to add a search engine or a dedicated vector database, and each adds a second copy of the content that must be kept in step, including its deletions and its entitlements.

Anthropic and Microsoft have published measurements for two of the six. The architectures act at three points. Contextual chunks are an index-time choice, and changing it re-embeds the corpus. Metadata filters, decomposition and multi-query act on the question before the search. Parent-child acts on the results after ranking. A change at query time can be switched per question type and rolled back in minutes. An index-time change cannot.

The lab judges relevance at the level of a section of a document, with two grades: 2 when the section answers the question and 1 when it is related and does not answer it. Judging at section level means the gold set stays valid when the chunking changes. Evidence sufficiency is checked with short phrases recorded for each question, such as "5,001 to 20,000" and "900", which must all appear in the returned text from the right document. The check is strict and cheap. Its weakness is that a phrase can be present in a passage that a reader would still find confusing.

High recall with low sufficiency means the right sections are found through the wrong chunks, which points at chunking or parent-child. High sufficiency with low nDCG means the evidence is there and badly ordered, which matters when a person reads the list and matters less when a model reads all five. Report several metrics and say which one the decision rests on. For an assistant that answers from the top five, that is evidence sufficiency.

Building a gold set

A is a list of questions with the passages that answer them, judged by people who know the content. Five rules decide whether it measures what users experience.

  1. Take questions from where they are asked: search logs, call notes, the questions new agents ask their team lead. A question written by someone looking at the passage borrows its words, and full-text search then looks better than it is.
  2. Cover the kinds of question deliberately. The lab's set has eight categories, including exact identifiers, vocabulary mismatches, table lookups, two-part questions, version-sensitive questions and questions the asker is not entitled to have answered.
  3. Include questions with no valid answer. Five of the lab's 60 have none: two ask about a withdrawn offer and three are asked by a role that may not read the answer. The correct result is that nothing forbidden or withdrawn is returned.
  4. Record the asker's role, the relevant sections with grades, and the evidence phrases. Have a second person judge a sample and discuss the disagreements. They are usually ambiguities in the question.
  5. Version the set with the corpus. When version 4 of the Gold card policy took effect, three answers changed. A gold set that still expects INR 750 punishes the system for being right.

What 60 questions can and cannot tell you

A metric over 60 questions is an estimate from a sample, and another 60 questions would give another number. A confidence interval expresses this: resample the per-question results with replacement 2,000 times, compute the mean each time, and report the range that holds the middle 95 percent of those means. In the baseline run for this page, recall@5 was 0.909 with an interval of 0.827 to 0.973, and evidence sufficiency 0.836 with 0.745 to 0.927, over 55 answerable questions. The intervals are about 15 and 18 points wide.

The course's evaluation plan sets an acceptance threshold for retrieval of recall@5 at 0.85 or higher on this gold set. The baseline's 0.909 passes. Its interval starts at 0.827, below the threshold, so a second set of 55 questions could plausibly fail. A threshold should be read against the interval, and a result this close to it is a reason to enlarge the set before signing off.

Case study: bank product-policy search for contact-centre staff

A fictional teaching case. Lakemont Bank, its staff, its incidents and its policies are invented. The measurements quoted are real: they come from running the lab's corpus and gold set for this page.

The situation

Lakemont Bank's contact centre answers questions about savings accounts, deposits, cards and loans. The rules are in 23 policy and process documents that the product teams re-issue several times a year. Agents searched them through a document portal with keyword search, and when the search failed they asked a team lead, with the customer on hold. A pilot replaced the portal's search with vector search over the documents, split into fixed-size pieces. Agents liked it for a month. Then three things happened.

What the measurement showed

With those decisions in place the baseline, on the 55 questions that have an answer the asker may read, returned sufficient evidence in the top five for 0.836 of them, with a 95 percent interval of 0.745 to 0.927. Nine questions failed. Reading them one at a time showed three patterns. Paragraphs such as "No closure charge applies at any time" never name their product, so the same paragraph of a sibling product outranked the right one. Several right sections were found through the introductory paragraph while the table with the amount was left out. And questions naming two products returned evidence for one.

The team switched on contextual headers and parent-child retrieval. Evidence sufficiency rose to 0.982, a paired difference of +0.145 with a 95 percent interval of +0.055 to +0.236. The price was a re-embedding of every chunk, which costs little at this size, and about 274 words returned per question in place of 230, which will matter when an answering model reads them.

The metadata filter was tried and rejected for now: it fixed several of the same failures and removed the answer for a question about a process that applies to a product. The decision record states that the interval on 55 questions is wide, that the project team wrote the questions with the policies in view, and that the comparison will be repeated when 200 logged questions have been judged.

Questions for discussion

  1. The second incident needed two faults. Which control would you test first, and how would you test it without a relevance judgement?
  2. Complaints handlers need to know what the late payment charge was on a past date. Which of the three version designs in section 5.2 serves them, and what changes in the query?
  3. The metadata filter was rejected because of one question in 55. Is that enough evidence? What would you need to see to adopt it for a subset of questions?
  4. The bank's information security team will not allow policy text to be sent to a hosted embedding API. What changes in the architecture, and what would you measure before agreeing that quality is unaffected?

Recap and checkpoint quiz

The table summarises the module. The quiz has ten scenario questions and, together with the review of the decision records from lab 6, is the formative check for Module 5.

IdeaSummary
Five different thingsSimilarity is a number from the engine. Relevance is a person's judgement of one passage. Evidence sufficiency is a property of the returned set. Factual correctness compares the answer with the world, and faithfulness compares it with the passages. Retrieval owns the first three.
IngestionKeep tables whole with column names on every row. Tag chunks with glossary terms and index the synonyms. Keep one current version in the search index. Refresh by content hash, delete withdrawn documents, and make every chunk inherit its document's audience, refusing documents that have none.
Embedding modelsDecide where text may go, then measure candidates on your own gold set. Dimension sets storage and index limits: pgvector indexes the vector type to 2,000 dimensions and halfvec to 4,000. A change of model re-embeds everything.
Hybrid retrievalFull-text search finds exact tokens and vector search finds paraphrases. Reciprocal rank fusion combines positions, never scores. A cross-encoder reranks the top candidates at a cost in latency. One PostgreSQL holds all of it in one transaction with one entitlement filter.
ArchitecturesMetadata-aware, decomposition and multi-query act on the question. Contextual chunks act at index time. Parent-child acts on results. Graph-assisted retrieval needs a graph. Each fixes one measured failure and has a cost.
EvaluationJudge relevance at section level, record evidence phrases and the asker's role, and include questions with no valid answer. Report intervals. Sixty questions separate large differences and catch regressions. They do not resolve small differences, rate categories or prove the absence of leaks.
Failure analysisFor each failed question find the stage that lost the evidence: ingestion, candidates, fusion, reranking, chunking, or the gold label. Change what that stage needs, run the same questions again and compare the paired difference.
